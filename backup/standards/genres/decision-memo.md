# Decision Memo Standard

Apply this standard when writing or reviewing:

- decision memos;
- recommendation memos;
- executive proposals;
- option analyses;
- investment or prioritization recommendations;
- written narratives whose primary purpose is to obtain or record a decision.

The purpose of a decision memo is to make a consequential choice understandable and decidable.

It should help the reader understand:

- what decision is needed;
- why it matters now;
- what evidence and constraints shape the decision;
- what realistic options exist;
- what is recommended;
- why;
- what the consequences are.

Do not treat this as a mandatory section template. Organize the memo according to the decision and audience.

## 1. Make the decision clear

### DM-01 — State the decision being requested

The reader should be able to identify the decision that needs to be made.

Do not make the reader infer the decision from background discussion.

A decision ask should be specific enough that the decision-maker knows what approving, rejecting, or choosing means.

### DM-02 — Put the recommendation early when appropriate

For an audience reviewing an already-developed recommendation, state the proposed direction early.

Then provide the reasoning necessary to evaluate it.

Do not hide the recommendation until the final paragraph merely to create narrative suspense.

If the purpose is genuinely exploratory rather than recommendatory, make that clear instead.

## 2. Establish why the decision exists

### DM-03 — Explain the decision context

Give the reader enough context to understand:

- the problem or opportunity;
- current state;
- relevant constraints;
- why a decision is necessary.

Do not turn the memo into a chronological history unless that history materially affects the decision.

### DM-04 — Explain why the decision matters now

When timing is consequential, explain the trigger.

Examples can include:

- a new requirement;
- operational pain;
- customer demand;
- cost;
- risk;
- a dependency;
- an upcoming commitment;
- new evidence;
- a strategic opportunity.

Do not manufacture urgency when no meaningful time pressure exists.

## 3. Frame the problem before evaluating solutions

### DM-05 — Define the problem independently of the preferred solution

Describe the need or constraint in a way that remains valid even if the recommendation changes.

Avoid framing the problem so narrowly that the preferred option becomes true by definition.

### DM-06 — Identify the decision criteria

Make the important evaluation criteria visible when they determine the choice.

Examples can include:

- customer impact;
- technical feasibility;
- cost;
- time to value;
- operational complexity;
- risk;
- reversibility;
- strategic fit;
- scalability;
- organizational capacity.

Do not create a generic scoring framework when the choice can be explained more clearly in prose.

## 4. Ground the argument in evidence

### DM-07 — Support consequential claims

Use the strongest relevant evidence available.

Evidence may include:

- measurements;
- experiments;
- customer evidence;
- financial analysis;
- operational data;
- research;
- historical outcomes;
- technical constraints;
- observed failures.

Do not substitute adjectives for evidence.

Prefer:

`The current process requires three manual handoffs and adds a median 1.8 days.`

over:

`The current process is highly inefficient.`

### DM-08 — Distinguish evidence from judgment

Make it possible to tell the difference between:

- known fact;
- measurement;
- assumption;
- forecast;
- interpretation;
- recommendation.

A recommendation can require judgment.

Do not present that judgment as if it were measured fact.

### DM-09 — Preserve uncertainty

State material uncertainty when it could change the decision.

Do not add defensive caveats around every claim.

Focus on uncertainty that affects:

- expected outcome;
- cost;
- feasibility;
- timing;
- risk;
- reversibility.

## 5. Present meaningful options

### DM-10 — Include realistic alternatives

Present alternatives that a reasonable decision-maker could actually choose.

Do not invent weak alternatives merely to make the recommendation look stronger.

### DM-11 — Include the status quo when meaningful

When continuing the current approach is a legitimate choice, make its consequences explicit.

Do not assume that making no change has zero cost or zero risk.

### DM-12 — Compare options using the decision criteria

Compare alternatives on the dimensions that actually matter to the decision.

Do not create long feature matrices containing differences that do not affect the choice.

## 6. Explain the recommendation

### DM-13 — State the recommendation unambiguously

The memo should identify the preferred option and what action it implies.

Avoid conclusions such as:

`Option B appears to be the best path forward.`

when the memo can say precisely what should be approved or done.

### DM-14 — Explain why this option wins

Connect the recommendation directly to:

- evidence;
- requirements;
- constraints;
- tradeoffs;
- decision criteria.

The reasoning should be strong enough that another reader can understand the choice even if they disagree with it.

### DM-15 — Explain the decisive factors

Not every difference between options deserves equal attention.

Identify what actually determines the recommendation.

A useful memo distinguishes decisive considerations from secondary advantages.

## 7. Make tradeoffs explicit

### DM-16 — Acknowledge what the recommendation gives up

A credible recommendation should communicate meaningful disadvantages, costs, or limitations.

Do not write advocacy that presents the preferred option as having only benefits.

### DM-17 — Explain why the tradeoff is acceptable

Where the recommendation accepts a disadvantage, explain why that cost is justified relative to the alternatives.

### DM-18 — Avoid false precision

Do not assign arbitrary numerical scores or weights simply to make a judgment appear objective.

Use quantified comparison when the quantities are meaningful and defensible.

## 8. Address consequences

### DM-19 — Explain what changes if the decision is approved

Describe the important consequences.

Depending on the decision, these can include:

- cost;
- ownership;
- staffing;
- architecture;
- process;
- schedule;
- dependencies;
- operational burden;
- customer impact;
- future constraints.

### DM-20 — Identify important downstream commitments

Some decisions create obligations that are difficult to reverse.

Make those commitments visible.

Do not discuss every implementation detail unless it affects whether the decision should be made.

## 9. Address risk

### DM-21 — Focus on decision-relevant risks

Identify risks that could materially change the expected outcome.

For important risks, communicate when useful:

- what could happen;
- why it matters;
- likelihood or uncertainty;
- mitigation;
- residual risk.

Do not create a generic risk register merely because the memo concerns an important decision.

### DM-22 — Distinguish reversible and irreversible decisions

When reversibility materially affects the decision, state it.

A reversible decision can justify:

- faster experimentation;
- lower evidence thresholds;
- staged commitment.

A difficult-to-reverse decision generally requires stronger evidence and clearer treatment of consequences.

Do not force this distinction when reversibility is irrelevant.

## 10. Make assumptions visible

### DM-23 — State assumptions that the recommendation depends on

An assumption belongs in the memo when the decision could change if that assumption proves false.

Do not list obvious background assumptions.

### DM-24 — Identify decision-changing assumptions

Where practical, explain:

> If this assumption changes, does the recommendation still hold?

This helps distinguish robust recommendations from fragile ones.

## 11. Keep the narrative coherent

### DM-25 — Build one decision argument

The memo should read as one connected line of reasoning:

`context → problem → evidence and constraints → options → tradeoffs → recommendation → consequences`

The exact section order may vary.

Do not write disconnected sections that force the reader to assemble the argument.

### DM-26 — Prefer narrative for reasoning

Use prose when explaining:

- causality;
- judgment;
- tradeoffs;
- rationale;
- implications.

Use tables when they make genuinely parallel comparison easier.

Do not replace reasoning with a large comparison table.

### DM-27 — Keep supporting detail subordinate

Place the evidence required to understand the recommendation in the main narrative.

Move deep supporting detail to an appendix or reference when it would interrupt the decision path.

Do not hide evidence essential to the recommendation in an appendix.

## 12. Optimize for the decision-maker

### DM-28 — Respect the reader's decision context

Prioritize information according to what the decision-maker needs to evaluate the choice.

Senior readers generally need:

- decision;
- rationale;
- material evidence;
- tradeoffs;
- consequences;
- risk;
- required action.

Do not include implementation detail merely to demonstrate depth.

### DM-29 — Make disagreement possible

A strong memo exposes enough reasoning that reviewers can identify exactly where they disagree.

Do not use vague language that makes the recommendation impossible to challenge.

### DM-30 — Make the requested action explicit

Where a decision is being requested, make clear what should happen next.

Examples:

- approve the recommended option;
- choose between two alternatives;
- authorize an experiment;
- allocate funding;
- defer the decision until specified evidence exists.

Avoid ending with an ambiguous request to `provide feedback` when a concrete decision is actually needed.

## 13. Keep the memo proportionate

### DM-31 — Optimize for decision density

Every substantial section should contribute to understanding or making the decision.

Do not expand the memo to achieve an arbitrary sense of completeness.

### DM-32 — Use page limits as forcing functions, not universal rules

If the organization or requester specifies a page limit, respect it.

Otherwise, choose the shortest length that adequately establishes the decision and its reasoning.

A six-page narrative is one useful organizational convention, not a universal requirement.

## 14. Record the decision faithfully

### DM-33 — Distinguish proposal from approved decision

Before approval, describe the recommendation as proposed.

After a decision has been made, record:

- the decision;
- important context;
- rationale;
- consequential tradeoffs or consequences.

Do not rewrite historical uncertainty as though the outcome had always been obvious.

### DM-34 — Preserve superseded decisions when relevant

When the artifact serves as a durable decision record, preserve enough history to understand when a prior decision was replaced and why.

Do not silently overwrite consequential historical decisions.

## Review expectation

When reviewing a decision memo, prioritize whether the reader can answer:

1. What decision is being made?
2. Why is the decision needed?
3. What evidence and constraints matter?
4. What realistic alternatives exist?
5. What is recommended?
6. Why does it win?
7. What does the recommendation give up?
8. What risks or assumptions could change the choice?
9. What happens if it is approved?
10. What action is required from the reader?

Do not require every memo to contain headings corresponding to these questions.

Evaluate whether the decision argument itself is complete, grounded, and easy to inspect.

# Universal Writing Standard

Apply this standard to every artifact unless a higher-priority writing requirement, genre standard, or channel standard requires something different.

The goal is not to impose one voice. The goal is to make the writing clear, purposeful, specific, and proportionate to what the reader needs.

## 1. Write for the reader

### UW-01 — Match the audience

Assume only the knowledge reasonably expected of the stated audience.

Explain concepts, acronyms, terminology, or context when the audience may not know them.

Do not explain material the audience can reasonably be expected to know unless the explanation serves another requirement.

### UW-02 — Include what the reader needs

Select information according to the artifact's communicative goal.

Do not include information merely because it is available.

Prefer the minimum sufficient explanation that lets the reader understand, decide, or act correctly.

## 2. Lead with substance

### UW-03 — Put important information early

Lead with the conclusion, decision, result, problem, or other information most important to the reader when the artifact allows it.

Do not make the reader pass through a generic introduction before reaching the substantive point.

### UW-04 — Make the purpose apparent

The reader should be able to understand why a section or paragraph exists from its content and placement.

Do not add sections simply because they are conventional.

## 3. Be specific

### UW-05 — Prefer concrete language

Prefer specific nouns, verbs, quantities, conditions, and relationships over vague abstractions.

Write what happened, what changes, what matters, or what must be done.

Avoid unsupported phrases such as:

* `This is important because...`
* `This provides significant value...`
* `This plays a crucial role...`

unless the writing immediately establishes the specific reason.

### UW-06 — Use precise verbs

Prefer verbs that state the actual action.

For example, prefer:

`The router selects a model.`

over:

`The router is responsible for the selection of a model.`

Do not mechanically eliminate nominalizations when the noun form is the natural technical term.

## 4. Keep terminology stable

### UW-07 — Use one term for one concept

Once a term has been established for a concept, continue using it unless there is a substantive reason to change.

Do not rotate synonyms merely to create variety.

If two terms have different meanings, make the distinction explicit.

### UW-08 — Avoid ambiguous references

Make pronouns and references clear enough that the reader can identify what they refer to without reconstructing the sentence.

Repeat a noun when repetition improves clarity.

## 5. Make sentences easy to process

### UW-09 — Keep each sentence focused

Prefer one primary idea or relationship per sentence.

Split a sentence when several independent ideas make it difficult to understand.

Do not shorten sentences mechanically when the ideas belong together and remain clear.

### UW-10 — Prefer direct constructions

Use active voice when it makes the actor and action clearer.

Use passive voice when the actor is irrelevant, unknown, intentionally de-emphasized, or when the object is the useful focus.

Clarity determines the choice.

### UW-11 — Remove unnecessary words

Remove words, phrases, and clauses that do not add:

* information;
* reasoning;
* qualification;
* navigation;
* emphasis that the artifact genuinely needs.

Conciseness means removing unnecessary content, not removing necessary explanation.

## 6. Build focused paragraphs

### UW-12 — Give each paragraph a job

A paragraph should develop one coherent topic, point, or step in the reasoning.

Move or remove sentences that do not contribute to that purpose.

### UW-13 — Establish the point early

When practical, make the paragraph's main point apparent near its beginning and use the remaining sentences to explain, support, qualify, or develop it.

Do not force this pattern when narrative or argumentative sequencing requires another structure.

### UW-14 — Avoid empty paragraphs

A paragraph should contribute meaningful information, reasoning, evidence, qualification, navigation, or intentional rhetorical function.

If removing the paragraph changes nothing meaningful for the reader, remove or consolidate it.

## 7. Control repetition

### UW-15 — Repeat for purpose, not emphasis by default

Do not restate the same idea using different wording merely to make the artifact appear complete or polished.

Repeat information when repetition helps:

* navigation;
* comprehension;
* decision making;
* deliberate summary;
* a genre-specific requirement.

### UW-16 — Do not summarize automatically

Do not add a recap, conclusion, or `In summary` section unless it provides value for the audience or is required by the artifact.

## 8. Use structure deliberately

### UW-17 — Let structure reflect relationships

Use headings, paragraphs, lists, tables, and other structures according to the information being communicated.

Do not turn ordinary prose into excessive headings or bullets merely for visual structure.

### UW-18 — Use lists when items are genuinely parallel

Use numbered lists when order or sequence matters.

Use bullets when items are parallel and order does not matter.

Keep list items grammatically and conceptually parallel where practical.

Do not use a list when the relationships between ideas are better communicated in prose.

## 9. Preserve logical relationships

### UW-19 — Use transitions only when they carry meaning

Use transitions to express real relationships such as:

* cause;
* contrast;
* consequence;
* sequence;
* qualification;
* continuation.

Do not insert generic transitions merely to make prose appear smooth.

### UW-20 — State relationships explicitly

When the reader needs to understand why two facts belong together, state the relationship rather than placing them next to each other and expecting the reader to infer it.

## 10. Control information density

### UW-21 — Match detail to purpose

The amount of detail must be appropriate for:

* the communicative goal;
* audience;
* scope;
* artifact type;
* explicit length constraints.

More available context does not justify a longer artifact.

### UW-22 — Do not over-explain

Stop explaining when the reader has enough information to understand, decide, or act at the level required by the task.

Do not add background, examples, definitions, caveats, or implications solely to maximize apparent completeness.

### UW-23 — Do not under-explain consequential points

Conciseness must not remove information required to understand:

* a decision;
* important reasoning;
* a constraint;
* a dependency;
* a risk;
* an uncertainty;
* an action.

## 11. Write naturally

### UW-24 — Prefer straightforward human language

Use language that is direct, natural, and appropriate for the audience.

Avoid unnecessary:

* jargon;
* inflated formality;
* marketing language;
* clichés;
* idioms;
* ornamental phrasing.

Technical terminology is appropriate when it is the clearest language for the audience.

### UW-25 — Do not optimize for superficial variation

Do not deliberately vary terminology, sentence structure, paragraph length, or wording simply to make the writing appear more human.

Variation should follow meaning and context.

## 12. Preserve qualification and uncertainty

### UW-26 — Match certainty to evidence

Use wording that reflects the actual strength of the available evidence.

Do not turn:

* a possibility into a fact;
* an observation into a general rule;
* a correlation into causation;
* a hypothesis into a conclusion;
* an unresolved issue into a resolved one.

### UW-27 — Keep consequential caveats

Preserve limitations, conditions, exceptions, and uncertainty when removing them would materially change the meaning.

Do not keep low-value caveats that merely weaken otherwise well-supported statements.

## 13. Make every part earn its place

Before keeping a sentence, paragraph, section, example, or aside, ask:

> What would the reader lose if this disappeared?

Keep it when the answer is meaningful.

Remove, combine, or rewrite it when the reader would lose nothing important.

This test does not override legitimate rhetorical, narrative, or genre-specific functions.

## Review expectation

When reviewing against this standard, identify the specific rule ID when practical.

For example:

`UW-07 — terminology changes between "worker capability" and "worker ability" without establishing a distinction.`

Do not mark a draft as misaligned merely because another valid stylistic choice exists.

A finding requires a meaningful effect on clarity, precision, usefulness, fidelity, or unnecessary reader effort.

# X Standard

Apply this standard when the final artifact will be published on X.

This standard applies to:

- individual posts;
- quote posts;
- replies;
- short threads.

It governs adaptation to short-form public writing.

It does not prescribe a personality, posting strategy, or engagement formula.

The primary goal is to communicate one useful idea with high information density and minimal reader effort.

## 1. Start with a real point

### X-01 — Know what the post is saying

Every post should have an identifiable purpose.

It may:

- make a claim;
- share an observation;
- report a result;
- express a position;
- ask a genuine question;
- provide useful information;
- respond to another idea.

Do not post polished language without a substantive point.

### X-02 — Prefer one primary idea

A short post should normally communicate one central idea.

Do not compress several unrelated arguments into the same post merely to fit them into one publication.

If the idea requires several distinct steps, use a thread or a longer-form artifact.

### X-03 — Make the point understandable on its own

Unless the post is intentionally part of a conversation or thread, provide enough context for a reader encountering it independently.

Do not rely on hidden context that only the author knows.

## 2. Use the opening efficiently

### X-04 — Begin with substance

Use the opening for the:

- claim;
- finding;
- observation;
- question;
- tension;
- relevant context.

Do not spend scarce space introducing the fact that you are about to say something.

Prefer:

`Most router benchmarks measure model selection on fixed task sets. That misses the harder production problem: the task distribution changes.`

over:

`I've been thinking a lot lately about LLM routing, and I wanted to share an observation.`

### X-05 — Do not manufacture a hook

An opening can be interesting without being manipulative.

Avoid unsupported constructions such as:

- `Nobody is talking about this.`
- `This changes everything.`
- `Hot take:`
- `Unpopular opinion:`
- `You won't believe...`

when the framing contributes nothing to the substance.

### X-06 — Do not hide the point for suspense

Short-form professional writing should normally reveal its value quickly.

Do not force the reader through several setup lines merely to obtain the central claim.

## 3. Maximize information density

### X-07 — Make every phrase contribute

Space is limited.

Prefer words that contribute:

- information;
- reasoning;
- qualification;
- context;
- emphasis that genuinely matters.

Remove conversational scaffolding that contributes none of these.

### X-08 — Compress language, not meaning

Shorten wording without removing:

- important conditions;
- uncertainty;
- causal distinctions;
- necessary context.

Do not convert a careful claim into a misleading absolute simply to make it shorter.

### X-09 — Prefer concrete statements

Specific observations are usually stronger than abstract commentary.

Prefer:

`The learned router improved pass rate by 4.8 points but increased median cost by 17%.`

over:

`The new router delivered impressive improvements with some tradeoffs.`

### X-10 — Use precise verbs

Prefer direct actions and relationships.

Prefer:

`The router selects GPT-5 for 38% of tasks.`

over:

`GPT-5 ends up being utilized by the router for 38% of tasks.`

## 4. Preserve the author's actual position

### X-11 — Do not invent opinions

If the post expresses a judgment, it must reflect an actual position supported by:

- the request;
- grounded content;
- supplied reasoning.

Do not manufacture a provocative opinion because strong opinions perform well as short-form content.

### X-12 — Do not invent experience

Never create personal:

- experiences;
- conversations;
- achievements;
- failures;
- observations;

that were not supplied or grounded.

### X-13 — Scope claims honestly

Distinguish between:

- what happened in one experiment;
- what the author has repeatedly observed;
- what evidence suggests;
- what the author believes;
- what is established more broadly.

Do not turn a local observation into a universal rule.

## 5. Make technical posts useful

### X-14 — Preserve the technical insight

Do not remove the mechanism or distinction that makes a technical point valuable merely to make the post broadly accessible.

Simplify presentation, not substance.

### X-15 — Explain specialized terms only when needed

If the intended audience reasonably knows the terminology, use it.

If understanding the post depends on an uncommon term, provide enough context to make the claim intelligible.

Do not consume most of the post defining terminology that can be avoided entirely.

### X-16 — Distinguish result from interpretation

When space permits, keep the observation and interpretation logically distinct.

Example:

`Route A won 61% of tasks, but almost all of the gain came from debugging tasks. The aggregate score hides a strong task-type effect.`

### X-17 — Preserve consequential numbers

When a quantitative result drives the point, keep the relevant:

- value;
- comparison;
- denominator;
- unit;

when needed for correct interpretation.

Do not replace useful measurements with adjectives.

## 6. Use threads only when the idea requires them

### X-18 — Use a thread for a multi-step argument

A thread is appropriate when the idea cannot be communicated responsibly in one post because it requires:

- several findings;
- sequential reasoning;
- an explanation;
- evidence;
- multiple related observations.

Do not create a thread merely to stretch one short idea across many posts.

### X-19 — Give the thread one coherent purpose

All posts in a thread should contribute to the same argument or explanation.

Do not use a thread as a container for loosely related observations.

### X-20 — Make the first post establish the thread's value

The first post should make clear:

- what the thread concerns;
- the central claim or question;
- why the remaining posts are useful.

Do not use:

`A thread 🧵`

as a substitute for explaining the actual topic.

### X-21 — Give each thread post a function

Each post should contribute something distinct, such as:

- evidence;
- explanation;
- example;
- counterargument;
- implication;
- conclusion.

Do not repeatedly restate the central claim.

### X-22 — Preserve sequence when sequence matters

Order thread posts so the reader can follow the reasoning without reconstructing it.

A useful flow might be:

`claim → evidence → explanation → implication`

but no fixed thread structure is mandatory.

### X-23 — End when the argument is complete

Do not add extra posts simply to produce a longer thread.

A three-post thread is valid when three posts are sufficient.

## 7. Use replies as conversation

### X-24 — Respond to the actual point

A reply should engage with what the other post said.

Do not use replies primarily as opportunities to redirect attention to an unrelated topic.

### X-25 — Preserve enough context

When disagreement depends on a particular claim, make the relevant point identifiable.

Do not respond to a complex argument with a vague:

`This is wrong.`

without explaining the substantive disagreement.

### X-26 — Disagree with the claim, not the person

Focus on:

- evidence;
- reasoning;
- assumptions;
- definitions;
- tradeoffs.

Do not substitute personal characterization for argument.

### X-27 — Ask genuine questions

A question should request information, clarification, evidence, or perspective the author actually wants.

Do not disguise a rhetorical attack as a question.

## 8. Use quote posts deliberately

### X-28 — Add value when quoting another post

A quote post should contribute something beyond repeating the original.

It may:

- interpret;
- disagree;
- extend;
- contextualize;
- highlight a specific implication.

### X-29 — Represent the source fairly

Do not remove context in a way that materially changes what the quoted author meant.

When the distinction matters, inspect the source before characterizing it.

### X-30 — Attribute ideas appropriately

When the post depends on another person's:

- finding;
- framework;
- argument;
- chart;
- research;

preserve appropriate attribution.

Do not make borrowed ideas appear original.

## 9. Use links without outsourcing the point

### X-31 — Give the reader a reason to follow the link

When linking to a:

- paper;
- article;
- repository;
- report;
- product;
- project;

state the relevant takeaway or reason it matters.

Do not post a bare link when the communication requires interpretation.

### X-32 — Keep the post useful even when the link contains depth

The post should communicate its central purpose without requiring the reader to open the link merely to discover why it was shared.

### X-33 — Do not summarize the entire linked artifact

Select the insight relevant to the post.

The linked source can contain the deeper detail.

## 10. Use formatting sparingly

### X-34 — Use line breaks for actual structure

Line breaks may separate:

- claim from evidence;
- distinct steps;
- contrasting ideas.

Do not put every sentence or phrase on its own line merely to occupy more visual space.

### X-35 — Use lists when the items are genuinely parallel

A short list can efficiently communicate:

- findings;
- differences;
- steps;
- criteria.

Do not force prose into a numbered list merely because lists scan well.

### X-36 — Avoid decorative formatting

Do not overuse:

- emoji;
- symbols;
- arrows;
- capitalization;
- repeated punctuation;
- decorative Unicode.

Use them only when they contribute meaning or useful navigation.

### X-37 — Do not use hashtags mechanically

Use a hashtag when it genuinely helps identify a topic, event, or conversation.

Do not append generic or unrelated hashtags solely in an attempt to increase distribution.

## 11. Keep the tone natural

### X-38 — Write like a person with something specific to say

Short-form writing can be:

- conversational;
- analytical;
- technical;
- humorous;
- direct;
- opinionated.

The appropriate tone depends on the author's actual voice and purpose.

Do not convert every idea into the same polished social-media voice.

### X-39 — Avoid inflated importance

Do not automatically describe developments as:

- huge;
- insane;
- groundbreaking;
- game-changing;
- revolutionary;
- massive;

unless the claim is justified.

### X-40 — Avoid unnecessary meta-commentary

Prefer:

`Claude's failure rate doubled on the adversarial subset.`

over:

`I wanted to share an interesting thing I noticed while looking at the results.`

when the framing contributes nothing.

## 12. Avoid short-form AI slop

### X-41 — Do not create fake profundity

Avoid transforming an ordinary observation into a generalized life lesson without evidence.

For example, do not automatically turn:

`The experiment failed because the evaluation set was too small.`

into:

`A powerful reminder that in AI, the quality of your questions matters more than the answers.`

### X-42 — Avoid formulaic contrasts

Constructions such as:

`It's not X. It's Y.`

`X isn't the problem. Y is.`

can be useful when they express a real distinction.

Do not use them automatically to make statements sound sharper.

### X-43 — Avoid repetitive punch lines

Do not state the same idea several times in increasingly short sentences.

Example of unnecessary repetition:

`The benchmark is broken.

Not imperfect.

Broken.`

unless the rhetorical emphasis genuinely serves the author's intended voice.

### X-44 — Avoid generic closing wisdom

Do not append statements such as:

- `The future belongs to those who adapt.`
- `Interesting times ahead.`
- `We're only getting started.`
- `The possibilities are endless.`

when they add no new meaning.

### X-45 — Do not force every post into a hot take

A useful:

- fact;
- observation;
- question;
- link;
- technical result;

does not need controversy or an aggressive opinion to justify publication.

## 13. Do not write for engagement bait

### X-46 — Do not manufacture interaction

Avoid constructions such as:

- `Agree?`
- `Thoughts?`
- `Who else thinks this?`
- `Like if you agree.`
- `Repost this if...`

when the primary purpose is to solicit engagement rather than advance a discussion.

### X-47 — Ask for discussion only when useful

A closing question is appropriate when the author genuinely wants:

- evidence;
- examples;
- disagreement;
- experience;
- alternative interpretations.

A post does not require a question at the end.

### X-48 — Do not exploit unrelated trends

Do not attach an unrelated trending topic, hashtag, person, or event to a post merely to obtain attention.

## 14. Handle announcements directly

### X-49 — State what happened

For an announcement, identify the concrete development early.

Examples:

- paper released;
- project launched;
- experiment completed;
- feature shipped;
- milestone reached.

### X-50 — Explain why it is worth knowing

When useful, add one meaningful:

- result;
- technical insight;
- capability;
- implication;
- lesson.

Do not turn a straightforward announcement into exaggerated promotional language.

### X-51 — Keep gratitude proportionate

Thank people when appropriate.

Do not replace information about the work with a long sequence of generic acknowledgments.

## 15. Handle research and paper posts carefully

### X-52 — State the contribution precisely

For research, distinguish between:

- what the paper proposes;
- what it evaluates;
- what it finds;
- what the author of the post concludes.

Do not compress these into one exaggerated novelty claim.

### X-53 — Report the relevant evidence

When citing a result, preserve enough context to understand what was measured.

Do not cherry-pick a favorable number that misrepresents the overall evidence.

### X-54 — Do not exaggerate novelty

Avoid claims such as:

`First ever...`

`Solves...`

`Proves...`

unless the underlying evidence supports them.

## 16. Preserve credibility

### X-55 — Verify public claims

Before publishing, verify consequential:

- facts;
- names;
- numbers;
- quotations;
- source attribution;
- links.

Public brevity does not reduce the standard for factual accuracy.

### X-56 — Match certainty to evidence

Short character limits do not justify removing important uncertainty.

Use precise qualification where it changes the claim.

### X-57 — Preserve confidentiality and privacy

Do not reveal confidential, proprietary, private, or sensitive information merely because specificity would make the post more compelling.

## 17. Choose the right artifact

### X-58 — Do not force long reasoning into short-form writing

When an argument requires substantial:

- evidence;
- qualification;
- technical detail;
- explanation;

write a longer artifact and use X to communicate the relevant takeaway.

### X-59 — Prefer a single post when one is sufficient

Do not create a thread when one concise post can communicate the idea accurately.

### X-60 — Prefer silence over filler

Not every event, result, or thought needs a post.

If there is no useful point to communicate, do not manufacture one merely to maintain posting frequency.

## Review expectation

When reviewing X writing, prioritize:

1. substantive value;
2. clarity of the central idea;
3. information density;
4. specificity;
5. fidelity to evidence and authorship;
6. appropriate claim scope;
7. natural short-form language;
8. absence of unnecessary setup and repetition;
9. absence of clickbait or engagement bait;
10. appropriate use of a single post versus a thread.

Do not penalize a post merely because it lacks:

- a hook;
- a thread;
- hashtags;
- emoji;
- a closing question;
- a call to action;
- a provocative opinion.

The strongest X writing communicates something worth knowing in the shortest form that preserves its meaning.

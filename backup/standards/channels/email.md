# Email Standard

Apply this standard when the final artifact will be delivered as a professional or workplace email.

This standard governs how information should be adapted to the email channel.

It does not define one universal email voice.

Tone, formality, and relationship-specific language should follow the writing requirements and audience.

The primary goal is to let the recipient quickly understand:

- why they received the email;
- what matters;
- whether they need to do something;
- what happens next.

## 1. Make the purpose immediately visible

### EM-01 — Use a descriptive subject

The subject should communicate the actual topic of the email.

Prefer:

`Router POC — review needed by Friday`

over:

`Quick question`

or:

`Update`

When the email requires an action or decision, make that visible in the subject when useful.

### EM-02 — Keep the subject aligned with the message

Do not use urgency, importance, or action language that the body does not support.

If an existing thread changes to a materially different topic, use a subject that reflects the new topic when practical.

## 2. Put the important information first

### EM-03 — Lead with the reason for the email

The recipient should not have to read several paragraphs before learning why the message was sent.

Depending on the situation, lead with:

- the request;
- decision needed;
- important update;
- conclusion;
- issue;
- relevant result.

Necessary context can follow.

### EM-04 — Front-load decision-critical information

When the recipient is expected to make a decision, surface:

- what decision is needed;
- the recommendation when one exists;
- material constraints;
- deadline when relevant.

Do not bury the decision request at the end of a long explanation.

## 3. Keep context proportional

### EM-05 — Include enough context to make the message self-contained

The recipient should be able to understand the request or update without reconstructing missing assumptions.

Include context that materially affects interpretation.

Do not assume shorthand is clear merely because the sender knows the context.

### EM-06 — Do not reproduce the entire history

Email is not a repository for every detail related to the topic.

Include only the history needed to understand the current message.

Point to another artifact when deeper context already exists elsewhere.

## 4. Make requests explicit

### EM-07 — State exactly what you need

When requesting something, specify the action clearly.

Prefer:

`Please review sections 2 and 3 and send any blocking concerns by Thursday.`

over:

`Let me know what you think.`

when specific review is actually required.

### EM-08 — Identify the responsible person when necessary

If several people receive the email, make ownership of an action unambiguous.

Do not send a request to a group when it is unclear who should respond.

### EM-09 — State the deadline when it matters

If timing affects the work, include the actual deadline.

Prefer a concrete date or time when ambiguity is possible.

Do not manufacture deadlines merely to create urgency.

### EM-10 — Make the response easy

Where practical, structure the request so the recipient can respond directly.

Examples:

- approve / reject;
- choose A or B;
- answer one specific question;
- provide requested information;
- review identified sections.

Do not make the recipient infer what constitutes a useful response.

## 5. Separate information from action

### EM-11 — Distinguish FYI from action-required messages

If the email is informational, do not imply that a response is expected.

If action is required, make the action visible.

### EM-12 — Make action items easy to scan

When several actions or questions exist, separate them visually when useful.

For example:

1. Review the proposed API boundary.
2. Confirm whether the migration can begin on September 8.
3. Identify any blocking dependency.

Do not convert a single straightforward request into an unnecessary list.

## 6. Optimize for a one-pass read

### EM-13 — Keep the message focused

Prefer one primary communication purpose per email when practical.

A message can contain several related points, but they should support the same overall purpose.

Do not combine unrelated topics merely to reduce the number of emails.

### EM-14 — Use short, functional paragraphs

Email paragraphs should generally be easy to scan.

Break apart dense text when separate ideas, decisions, or actions are being communicated.

Do not mechanically make every paragraph one sentence long.

### EM-15 — Use structure only when it helps

Use:

- short paragraphs;
- bullets;
- numbered actions;
- small tables;

when they reduce recipient effort.

Do not over-format a short message.

## 7. Be concise without becoming cryptic

### EM-16 — Remove low-value setup

Avoid unnecessary openings such as extended background, generic pleasantries, or explanations that delay the actual message.

A brief social opening is appropriate when the relationship or situation calls for it.

### EM-17 — Do not optimize for minimum word count

A very short email is not automatically a good email.

Include enough information that the recipient does not need another exchange simply to understand:

- what happened;
- what is needed;
- why;
- by when.

### EM-18 — Move deep detail elsewhere when appropriate

If the email depends on substantial technical analysis, design detail, experiment results, or documentation, summarize the decision-relevant information and link or attach the full artifact.

Do not paste an entire report into an email unless the requirements call for it.

## 8. Preserve thread context

### EM-19 — Answer the questions actually asked

When replying to an email, account for each consequential question, request, or decision in the relevant thread context.

Do not produce a polished response that accidentally ignores one of the sender's requests.

### EM-20 — Avoid repeating settled context

Do not restate information already understood by everyone in the thread unless it is needed to frame a new decision or prevent ambiguity.

### EM-21 — Make changes in direction explicit

When a new message changes:

- a decision;
- requirement;
- deadline;
- owner;
- assumption;

state the change directly.

Do not make recipients detect the change by comparing messages manually.

## 9. Use recipients intentionally

### EM-22 — Send only to relevant recipients

Include people who need:

- the information;
- to take action;
- to make a decision;
- to remain appropriately informed.

Do not add recipients merely for visibility without a meaningful reason.

### EM-23 — Distinguish action recipients from awareness recipients

Where workplace conventions support it:

- `To` should primarily contain people expected to engage or act;
- `Cc` should primarily contain people who need awareness but are not expected to act.

Do not rely on recipient fields alone when ownership of an important action could still be ambiguous.

## 10. Match tone to the relationship

### EM-24 — Use professional, natural language

Write as a person communicating with another person.

Avoid unnecessary:

- corporate ceremony;
- exaggerated politeness;
- canned enthusiasm;
- marketing language;
- robotic transitions.

### EM-25 — Match formality to context

A message to:

- a close teammate;
- senior leadership;
- a customer;
- an unfamiliar external stakeholder;

may legitimately use different levels of formality.

Do not automatically make every workplace email formal.

### EM-26 — Be direct without being careless

Directness should reduce ambiguity, not remove necessary interpersonal judgment.

Avoid wording that can reasonably be interpreted as:

- hostile;
- dismissive;
- accusatory;
- demanding beyond the actual relationship.

Do not weaken clear requests with excessive hedging merely to sound polite.

## 11. Use greetings and closings proportionally

### EM-27 — Do not over-engineer the greeting

Use a greeting appropriate to the relationship and situation.

In an active internal thread, a new formal greeting may be unnecessary.

In a first external message, one may be appropriate.

### EM-28 — Keep the closing functional

Use a closing when it serves the communication convention or relationship.

Do not add a long ceremonial closing after a short operational message.

If an action is required, the final substantive line should leave that action clear.

## 12. Handle updates clearly

### EM-29 — Lead status updates with the current state

For a status email, prioritize:

- current status;
- meaningful progress;
- blockers or risks;
- next step;
- required decision or assistance.

Do not narrate every activity performed since the previous update.

### EM-30 — Separate status from risk

If something threatens:

- scope;
- quality;
- cost;
- schedule;
- dependency;

make the risk visible rather than hiding it inside routine progress text.

### EM-31 — State changed expectations explicitly

If a previous commitment can no longer be met, state:

- what changed;
- resulting impact;
- proposed response;

when known.

Do not obscure a delay or problem with optimistic wording.

## 13. Handle decision emails clearly

### EM-32 — State the decision needed

When the email exists to obtain approval or direction, make the decision explicit.

### EM-33 — Provide only decision-relevant reasoning

Summarize the evidence and tradeoffs required for the recipient to make the decision.

Link to the full decision memo or supporting artifact when deeper analysis exists.

### EM-34 — State your recommendation when you have one

Do not force the recipient to derive your position from a list of options.

Distinguish clearly between:

- the evidence;
- your recommendation;
- the decision the recipient owns.

## 14. Handle disagreement clearly

### EM-35 — Identify the substantive disagreement

Focus on the:

- requirement;
- assumption;
- evidence;
- decision;
- tradeoff;

rather than characterizing the person.

### EM-36 — Separate fact from interpretation

When disagreement depends on different interpretations, make the distinction visible.

Avoid escalating disagreement through unnecessarily absolute language.

### EM-37 — Move out of email when the medium stops helping

If a thread becomes difficult to resolve because of:

- repeated misunderstanding;
- complex negotiation;
- high emotional sensitivity;
- rapid back-and-forth;
- extensive unresolved disagreement;

a synchronous conversation may be more effective.

When moving channels, preserve any resulting decision in an appropriate durable form.

## 15. Avoid common email slop

### EM-38 — Do not begin with generic filler

Avoid automatically generated openings such as:

`I hope this email finds you well.`

`I wanted to reach out regarding...`

`I just wanted to take a moment to...`

They are not forbidden when genuinely appropriate.

Do not use them as default padding before the real message.

### EM-39 — Avoid redundant closing language

Do not repeat the request in several increasingly polite forms.

For example, avoid:

`Please let me know your thoughts. I would really appreciate your feedback. Looking forward to hearing what you think.`

when one clear request is sufficient.

### EM-40 — Avoid vague corporate phrasing

Prefer the actual action or issue over phrases such as:

- `circle back`;
- `touch base`;
- `align on`;
- `connect regarding`;
- `move this forward`;

when those phrases conceal what specifically needs to happen.

Use them when they accurately and naturally describe the intended action.

## 16. Check before sending

### EM-41 — Verify the recipient can answer four questions

Before finalizing, check whether the recipient can quickly determine:

1. Why am I receiving this?
2. What do I need to know?
3. Do I need to do something?
4. If so, what and by when?

If any answer is consequential and unclear, revise the email.

### EM-42 — Verify factual and commitment accuracy

Check consequential:

- names;
- dates;
- numbers;
- links;
- attachments;
- commitments;
- requested actions.

Do not let stylistic polish hide an incorrect commitment or missing attachment.

## Review expectation

When reviewing an email, prioritize:

1. purpose clarity;
2. recipient relevance;
3. action or decision clarity;
4. necessary context;
5. information ordering;
6. concision without missing context;
7. appropriate tone;
8. scannability;
9. factual and commitment accuracy.

Do not penalize an email merely for being short, informal, or structurally simple when that form fits the relationship and purpose.

The strongest email is the one that lets the intended recipient understand and respond correctly with the least unnecessary effort.

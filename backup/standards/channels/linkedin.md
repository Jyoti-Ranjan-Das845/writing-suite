# LinkedIn Standard

Apply this standard when the final artifact will be published as a LinkedIn post or LinkedIn article.

This standard governs how professional ideas should be adapted to LinkedIn.

It does not define a personal brand or require one particular writing style.

The primary goal is to publish something that contributes a useful professional:

- insight;
- experience;
- argument;
- observation;
- lesson;
- result;
- question.

The writing should contain a real point of view or useful substance rather than polished language designed primarily to attract attention.

## 1. Have something worth saying

### LI-01 — Start from substance

Before writing the post, identify what the reader should learn, reconsider, understand, or discuss.

Do not create a post merely because there is an event, announcement, project, paper, or trend available to post about.

### LI-02 — Make the central point identifiable

A reader should be able to explain the main idea of the post in one sentence.

Do not combine several unrelated observations into one post simply to make it appear substantial.

### LI-03 — Prefer specific professional knowledge

Strong LinkedIn content should normally draw from something concrete, such as:

- work performed;
- an experiment;
- a technical insight;
- professional experience;
- a decision;
- a failure;
- an observation;
- research;
- a useful synthesis;
- a defensible opinion.

Do not replace missing substance with motivational language.

## 2. Preserve genuine perspective

### LI-04 — Represent the author's actual position

When the post expresses an opinion, the position should come from the grounded content or explicit authorship requirements.

Do not manufacture a strong opinion merely because opinionated writing may attract attention.

### LI-05 — Do not invent personal experience

Never create:

- experiences;
- conversations;
- failures;
- achievements;
- emotions;
- lessons learned;

that were not provided or grounded.

If the author did not personally experience something, write from the appropriate evidential position instead.

### LI-06 — Distinguish experience from generalization

A personal observation can be valuable without being universally true.

Prefer:

`In our evaluation, this failed when...`

over:

`This approach never works.`

unless broader evidence supports the stronger claim.

## 3. Open with the actual idea

### LI-07 — Make the opening earn attention

The first lines should establish something substantive:

- the finding;
- tension;
- question;
- surprising observation;
- position;
- result;
- problem.

The opening exists to orient the reader, not merely to create curiosity.

### LI-08 — Avoid empty hooks

Do not default to constructions such as:

`This changed everything.`

`I wasn't ready for what happened next.`

`Nobody is talking about this.`

`Here's the truth about AI.`

when the claim is unsupported or primarily designed to withhold the point.

### LI-09 — Do not delay the payoff artificially

Give readers enough information early to understand what the post is about.

Do not turn a straightforward professional insight into a suspense narrative solely for engagement.

## 4. Develop one coherent argument

### LI-10 — Move from point to support

After establishing the main point, develop it with the most useful supporting material.

A common useful flow is:

`observation → evidence or reasoning → implication`

or:

`problem → insight → consequence`

This is not a mandatory template.

Use whatever structure best represents the actual idea.

### LI-11 — Explain why the observation matters

When the significance is not obvious, connect the evidence to its implication.

Do not state:

`This is a game changer.`

Explain what changes and for whom.

### LI-12 — Keep side points subordinate

Do not allow related observations, background, or caveats to overwhelm the central idea.

If a side point deserves its own argument, it may deserve its own post.

## 5. Show rather than proclaim expertise

### LI-13 — Demonstrate expertise through substance

Use:

- concrete observations;
- technical reasoning;
- useful examples;
- evidence;
- specific lessons;
- informed interpretation.

Do not rely on self-descriptions such as:

`As a thought leader...`

`As an expert in this space...`

when the content itself can establish credibility.

### LI-14 — Prefer useful detail over prestige signals

Names, institutions, employers, conferences, awards, or credentials may be relevant context.

Do not make them substitute for the actual insight.

### LI-15 — Explain specialized material enough for the intended audience

Technical depth is appropriate when the audience benefits from it.

Do not remove useful technical detail merely to maximize broad accessibility.

At the same time, provide enough framing that the intended professional audience can follow the argument.

## 6. Make announcements substantive

### LI-16 — State what actually happened

For an announcement, establish the concrete development.

Examples:

- a project shipped;
- research was published;
- an experiment concluded;
- a new role started;
- a product launched;
- a milestone was reached.

### LI-17 — Add meaningful context

When useful, explain:

- what was difficult;
- what was learned;
- what is different;
- what the result enables;
- what remains unresolved.

Do not pad a simple announcement with generic reflections solely to make it longer.

### LI-18 — Use gratitude specifically

When thanking collaborators, identify genuine contributions when appropriate.

Avoid long ceremonial gratitude sections composed of interchangeable praise.

## 7. Write lessons carefully

### LI-19 — Ground lessons in the evidence

A lesson should follow from the experience, result, or reasoning described.

Do not manufacture a philosophical takeaway because professional posts conventionally end with one.

### LI-20 — Keep the scope of the lesson honest

Distinguish between:

- what happened once;
- what happened repeatedly;
- what the evidence suggests;
- what the author believes;
- what has been established more broadly.

### LI-21 — Avoid universal career aphorisms without support

Statements such as:

`Consistency always wins.`

`Your network is your net worth.`

`Failure is the best teacher.`

usually add little unless the post develops a specific and relevant insight around them.

## 8. Use evidence naturally

### LI-22 — Include evidence that strengthens the point

Useful evidence may include:

- measurements;
- experiment results;
- concrete examples;
- research;
- observed outcomes;
- comparisons.

Do not overload a short post with every supporting detail available.

### LI-23 — Attribute external ideas

When a consequential idea, result, quotation, chart, or argument comes from another source, preserve appropriate attribution.

Do not present another person's insight as the author's original observation.

### LI-24 — Keep numbers meaningful

Use numbers when they clarify the argument.

Explain the denominator, comparison, or context when necessary for correct interpretation.

Do not add statistics merely to make the post appear evidence-based.

## 9. Make formatting serve reading

### LI-25 — Use readable paragraphs

LinkedIn posts should be easy to read in a feed.

Break dense blocks when separate ideas or stages of reasoning exist.

Do not mechanically put every sentence on its own line.

### LI-26 — Use whitespace intentionally

Whitespace can make transitions and major points easier to process.

Do not create excessive vertical spacing merely to make a short idea occupy more screen space.

### LI-27 — Use bullets when information is genuinely parallel

Bullets work well for:

- findings;
- lessons;
- comparisons;
- steps;
- examples.

Do not convert connected reasoning into a list solely because lists are visually scannable.

### LI-28 — Use emphasis sparingly

Do not rely on:

- excessive capitalization;
- repeated punctuation;
- decorative symbols;
- emoji;
- artificial section markers;

to manufacture emphasis.

Meaning should carry most of the emphasis.

## 10. Keep the tone professional but human

### LI-29 — Write as a knowledgeable person, not a corporate announcement generator

Natural professional language may be:

- conversational;
- technical;
- reflective;
- opinionated;
- concise;
- analytical.

Choose according to the author and subject.

### LI-30 — Avoid inflated significance

Do not describe ordinary developments as:

- groundbreaking;
- revolutionary;
- transformative;
- incredible;
- game-changing;

unless the evidence justifies the description.

### LI-31 — Avoid manufactured humility

Do not automatically use constructions such as:

`Humbled and honored...`

`Still processing this...`

`I can't believe I get to say this...`

unless they genuinely represent the author's intended expression.

### LI-32 — Avoid manufactured vulnerability

Personal difficulty can be appropriate and valuable.

Do not invent or exaggerate vulnerability as a storytelling device.

## 11. Avoid AI-slop patterns

### LI-33 — Do not produce polished emptiness

A grammatically clean post can still fail if it contains:

- no meaningful claim;
- no specific experience;
- no useful evidence;
- no original synthesis;
- no defensible perspective.

Surface fluency is not sufficient.

### LI-34 — Remove generic significance statements

Flag statements that could be inserted into almost any professional post without changing their meaning.

Examples include:

`This experience taught me the importance of collaboration and perseverance.`

`The future is full of exciting possibilities.`

`Innovation happens when passionate people come together.`

Replace them only when grounded content supports something more specific.

### LI-35 — Avoid repetitive reframing

Do not make the same point repeatedly through:

- hook;
- explanation;
- bullet list;
- summary;
- final lesson;

unless each occurrence contributes something different.

### LI-36 — Avoid formulaic contrast for its own sake

Constructions such as:

`It's not about X. It's about Y.`

or:

`This isn't just X — it's Y.`

can be valid.

Do not use them automatically to make ordinary statements sound profound.

### LI-37 — Do not rotate synonyms for style

If the post establishes a technical or professional concept, keep the terminology stable.

Do not alternate between several near-synonyms merely to avoid repetition.

## 12. Do not optimize for engagement tricks

### LI-38 — Do not withhold information to force interaction

Avoid intentionally incomplete claims whose primary purpose is to generate comments.

### LI-39 — Do not manufacture controversy

A defensible disagreement or strong position is appropriate.

Do not exaggerate a position merely because polarization may increase engagement.

### LI-40 — Do not use engagement bait

Avoid requests such as:

`Agree?`

`Thoughts?`

`Comment YES if...`

`Repost if you agree.`

when they exist primarily to solicit engagement rather than advance the discussion.

### LI-41 — Ask a question only when you want an answer

A closing question should invite meaningful professional discussion.

It should not be a mandatory ending.

A strong post can simply end when the argument is complete.

## 13. Handle calls to action proportionally

### LI-42 — Match the call to action to the purpose

A call to action may legitimately ask readers to:

- read a paper;
- try a tool;
- review a project;
- apply for a role;
- attend something;
- share relevant experience.

State it directly.

### LI-43 — Do not force a call to action

If the purpose is simply to share an insight, no call to action is required.

Do not append:

`Follow me for more...`

or similar language automatically.

## 14. Distinguish posts from articles

### LI-44 — Use posts for focused ideas

A post is generally appropriate for a bounded:

- insight;
- result;
- announcement;
- observation;
- argument;
- question.

Keep the scope narrow enough to work in a feed.

### LI-45 — Use articles for deeper treatment

When the subject requires substantial:

- explanation;
- analysis;
- narrative;
- evidence;
- examples;

a LinkedIn article or another long-form artifact may be more appropriate.

Do not compress a full technical report into an oversized feed post when a linked artifact would communicate it better.

## 15. Preserve professional trust

### LI-46 — Take responsibility for the published claims

Verify consequential:

- facts;
- numbers;
- attributions;
- names;
- quotations;
- links.

The author remains responsible for the published content regardless of how it was drafted.

### LI-47 — Do not disguise generated substance as personal knowledge

AI may help organize or express supplied thinking.

It must not manufacture the author's:

- expertise;
- experiences;
- beliefs;
- observations;
- relationships;
- achievements.

### LI-48 — Preserve confidentiality

Do not reveal confidential, private, proprietary, or personally sensitive information merely because it would make the post more specific or interesting.

## 16. End when the idea is complete

### LI-49 — Let the conclusion follow from the post

A final line may:

- state the implication;
- state the lesson;
- identify what happens next;
- pose a genuine question;
- return to the central observation.

Do not manufacture an inspirational ending.

### LI-50 — Do not summarize unnecessarily

For a short post, repeating everything at the end usually adds little.

End once the argument has delivered its value.

## Review expectation

When reviewing LinkedIn writing, prioritize:

1. substantive professional value;
2. clear central point;
3. grounding in genuine experience, evidence, or perspective;
4. useful specificity;
5. appropriate scope;
6. coherent reasoning;
7. professional credibility;
8. readable feed formatting;
9. absence of engagement manipulation;
10. absence of generic or repetitive AI-slop language.

Do not optimize the post for a presumed platform algorithm.

Do not penalize a post merely because it lacks:

- a dramatic hook;
- a personal story;
- a list;
- emoji;
- a closing question;
- a call to action.

The strongest LinkedIn writing gives the professional reader something worth knowing while preserving the author's real perspective and credibility.
